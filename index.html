Course summary

A different starting point for STEM

Today’s students are capable, curious, and quick with technology. They care about climate change, pollution, unequal access to technology, and the resources we keep using without putting back.

School often rewards quick questions such as “How can we make this faster?” or “How can we make this cheaper?” ROOTS Quest asks students to slow down, look beneath the symptom, and understand the whole system behind a problem.

Over eight weeks, students learn to design the way nature does and create responses that help life thrive. That is regeneration: taking part in the place you live, strengthening relationships, and giving back so people and nature can grow healthier together. It can apply to a forest, a school cafeteria, a piece of technology, or an entire city.

The goal is not simply to do less harm. It is to make things better—and to send students home inspired, confident, and ready to create change where they live.

What you will learn

Ideas students can use—and a project that makes them real

The course combines new ways of thinking with the practical work of researching, mapping, making, and presenting.

  • What regenerative thinking and systems design actually mean—not just noticing connections, but tracing feedback, loops, scales, and leverage points.
  • How systems design and regenerative thinking strengthen one another and support change that can keep growing over time.
  • Why regeneration is larger than ecological sustainability: it is a creative, living process that can shape technology, communities, and everyday decisions.
  • How biomimicry and nature-inspired problem solving can guide STEM design.
  • Choose a real-world problem that matters personally and map the system behind it.
  • Design a next step: an experiment, prototype, campaign, or proposal.
  • Study real examples of organizations using systems and regenerative thinking today.
  • Share a finished systems map, process journal, prototype, and pitch.

Knowledge · 01

Systems design

What is it? Every problem sits inside a bigger system of people, places, technology and nature. Systems design is learning to map those connections: what causes what, which loops keep a problem going, and where a small change matters most.

  • Mapping systems and feedback loops
  • Connecting environment, technology and society
  • Finding real points of change
Knowledge · 02

Regenerative design

What is it? Many “green” goals aim to make things last, or be a little less bad. Regenerative design aims higher: to leave a system healthier than we found it, for ecosystems and for the people who depend on them.

  • Degenerative vs. regenerative thinking
  • Learning design from nature
  • Proposing nature-inspired solutions
Skills · 03

Working through a project

How we teach it. Each student picks a problem they genuinely care about and works it from first look to final pitch: observing closely, finding their own connections, asking their own question and building a first version.

  • Observing and noticing patterns
  • Finding and evaluating sources
  • Building a lo-fi prototype and pitching it

How a student’s project unfolds

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Observe

    Slow down and look closely at the world around them.

  2. Weeks 3–5

    Connect

    Trace how people, technology and nature link to the problem.

  3. Week 6

    Question

    Choose their own question worth answering.

  4. Week 7

    Build

    Prototype a first version and get honest feedback.

  5. Week 8

    Share

    Present to a live audience of parents and peers.

Every student leaves with four finished pieces of work:

  • Process Journal
  • Systems Map
  • Lo-Fi Prototype
  • Pitch Deck

Why take this course?

A way of thinking most students have not had the chance to meet in school

Instead of rushing toward a fix, students learn to ask why the problem is happening, who and what it affects, and where change could matter most.

Students practise noticing patterns, relationships, feedback, and the parts of a system that are easy to miss when looking for a quick answer.

Nature has been solving hard problems for billions of years. Students learn from living systems and design responses that help the larger system grow rather than simply hiding a symptom.

Students meet other young people who care about similar questions and discover they do not need to wait until adulthood to contribute meaningful ideas.

Seeing how things connect supports future learning, teamwork, design, and decision-making. Organizations such as Patagonia, Microsoft, and the United Nations use related approaches in real work today.

01

Beyond knowing the skills

Coding, building and lab skills are the starting point. The students who shape the future also ask what a design connects to, who it affects, and what it leaves behind.

02

Good for people and planet

Regenerative design isn’t only ecological. It means designing products, cities, tools and organizations that strengthen the communities and systems around them, not just cause less harm.

03

Already happening in industry

Organizations around the world, including Patagonia, Microsoft, the United Nations and the city of Singapore, are applying systems thinking and regenerative design.

Who it’s for

For students who are curious about how the world actually works

ROOTS Quest is designed for students in Grades 6⁠–⁠8 (ages 11–14). It may be a good fit if any of these sound familiar.

You enjoy STEM and want more room to explore ideas, make connections, and follow questions beyond the textbook.

You notice details, challenge assumptions, and enjoy finding out what sits behind the obvious answer.

You want to do something real about the issues around you, even if you are still discovering what your contribution could be.

You are ready to work on a question that matters to you—not one assigned from a textbook.

You have enjoyed school projects and now want to understand the system behind a problem, design a response, and explain your thinking to others.

Come learn to see the world the way a forest works: connected, resilient, and alive.

Science-fair students

You’ve done science fairs and loved them, and you’re ready for a project where the question is yours and the system behind it matters as much as the result.

Curious explorers

Builders with a specialty

You’re deep into one area of building, like aerospace, rovers or robotics, and you want to see how your skills could solve real, impactful problems out in the world.
